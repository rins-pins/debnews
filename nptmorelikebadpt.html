The NPT is failing, and the TPNW is the only solution

By Junior Reporter Jennifer Mirriam | October 2026

As global nuclear tensions rise, the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT) is facing unprecedented challenges. The NPT, established in 1968, was designed to prevent the spread of nuclear weapons and promote disarmament. However, recent developments have exposed its limitations and the urgent need for a more effective framework.

Today we talk about the NPT.

For more than half a century, the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT) has served as the foundation of the global nuclear order. Established in 1970, the treaty sought to prevent the spread of nuclear weapons, promote peaceful nuclear energy, and achieve eventual nuclear disarmament. Yet, despite its ambitious promises, the NPT has created a system in which nuclear-armed states retain their weapons while demanding that the rest of the world permanently renounce them. This unequal arrangement has undermined the treaty’s legitimacy and exposed the fundamental contradiction at the heart of international nuclear policy.

The world cannot achieve lasting security through a system that permits some nations to threaten mass destruction while prohibiting others from possessing the same weapons. The Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (TPNW) offers a necessary alternative: a framework built around the universal rejection of nuclear weapons and the recognition that their humanitarian consequences cannot be justified. The NPT must be replaced by the TPNW because its discriminatory structure, failure to achieve disarmament, and dependence on nuclear deterrence have made it an inadequate foundation for lasting global security.

An Unequal Treaty That Perpetuates Nuclear Privilege

The NPT’s most fundamental flaw is its institutionalized inequality. Under the treaty, five countries—the United States, Russia, China, France, and the United Kingdom—are recognized as nuclear-weapon states because they possessed nuclear weapons before January 1, 1967. These countries are permitted to retain their arsenals, while all other signatories are prohibited from developing or acquiring nuclear weapons. This distinction creates a permanent hierarchy between states that possess nuclear weapons and those that do not.

The NPT’s original bargain was that non-nuclear-weapon states would agree not to acquire nuclear weapons in exchange for the nuclear-weapon states pursuing disarmament. Article VI explicitly commits all parties to pursue negotiations in good faith toward nuclear disarmament. However, this obligation has not produced the elimination of nuclear arsenals. Instead, nuclear-armed states continue to modernize their weapons, maintain deterrence policies, and invest in increasingly sophisticated delivery systems. The treaty’s unequal obligations have consequently become more than a temporary compromise: they have evolved into a system that protects the nuclear privileges of powerful states.

This structural imbalance undermines the NPT’s credibility. Non-nuclear-weapon states are expected to demonstrate unwavering compliance with their obligations, while nuclear-weapon states are permitted to indefinitely postpone fulfilling theirs. As scholars have argued, this unequal distribution of responsibilities has contributed to the erosion of the treaty’s legitimacy. The NPT cannot credibly claim to establish a universal standard against nuclear weapons when its own structure explicitly permits their possession by a privileged group of states.

The NPT Has Failed to Deliver Nuclear Disarmament

The NPT’s failure is not simply a matter of inequality; it is also a matter of results. Nuclear disarmament remains one of the treaty’s central objectives, yet the world continues to depend on nuclear weapons as instruments of national security.

The NPT’s continued existence should not be mistaken for its success. A treaty may retain widespread membership while failing to achieve its most important objectives. Preventing additional states from acquiring nuclear weapons is valuable, but it cannot be considered sufficient when the treaty also promises to eliminate the weapons that already exist. The international community must move beyond a framework that manages nuclear inequality and toward one that actively dismantles it.

Iraq and Libya secretly pursued nuclear weapons programs in violation of their NPT obligations, while Iran has violated nuclear safeguards through undeclared activities. These cases expose the NPT’s inability to reliably prevent proliferation, as enforcement depends on detecting violations after they occur. The treaty’s failure to stop these programs demonstrates the need for a stronger framework like the TPNW that prohibits nuclear weapons entirely.

The TPNW: A Path Toward a Nuclear-Free World

Adopted in 2017 and entering into force in 2021, the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons represents a fundamentally different approach to nuclear security. Rather than accepting the indefinite possession of nuclear weapons by a select group of countries, the TPNW establishes a comprehensive prohibition against developing, possessing, using, threatening to use, transferring, or stationing nuclear weapons.

The TPNW therefore provides a more consistent framework for international security. It does not distinguish between states that are entitled to possess nuclear weapons and states that are prohibited from doing so. Instead, it establishes the principle that nuclear weapons should be eliminated entirely. This represents a necessary shift away from the NPT’s unequal system and toward a genuinely universal standard.

Nuclear Deterrence Is Not a Sustainable Foundation for Security

Defenders of the NPT frequently argue that nuclear weapons are necessary to maintain international stability. According to this perspective, nuclear deterrence prevents major wars by making the consequences of aggression too severe for any state to accept. However, this argument overlooks the fundamental danger of relying on weapons capable of destroying entire cities and causing catastrophic humanitarian consequences.

Nuclear deterrence depends on the assumption that states will always act rationally, accurately interpret one another’s intentions, and avoid accidental escalation. Yet international crises are frequently characterized by uncertainty, miscommunication, and political instability. The possibility of miscalculation means that nuclear deterrence cannot guarantee that nuclear weapons will never be used.

Furthermore, the continued reliance on nuclear deterrence encourages states to maintain and modernize their arsenals. This creates a security dilemma in which one country’s efforts to strengthen its nuclear capabilities provoke other countries to respond with their own military expansion. Rather than eliminating the threat of nuclear conflict, the NPT’s current framework allows this cycle to continue.
